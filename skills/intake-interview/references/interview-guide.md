# Interview guide

Question banks and elicitation moves. **These are raw material, not a script.** Never read them in order, never read them aloud as a list. Pick what fits what the person just said.

## Contents

- Stage 1 — setup facts
- Stage 2 — clinical baseline
- Stage 3 — regimen
- Stage 4 — norms elicitation (the six moves)
- Handling vagueness
- Stopping well

---

## Stage 1 — setup facts

- What should I call you?
- Where are you, roughly — I need the timezone so timestamps line up.
- Pounds or kilos? Fahrenheit or Celsius?
- Do you wear anything that tracks you — watch, ring, patch?
- Anything you measure with by hand? Blood pressure cuff, scale, thermometer, glucose meter?

**Then the question that actually matters:** *where does each one live, and when do you use it?*

A cuff kept by the bed and used lying down produces a different series than a cuff on a desk used sitting up. Same for a watch that comes off at night, a scale used at different times of day, a glucose meter used only when they feel bad. Get the physical arrangement. It is the difference between a readable series and noise.

If they have two of the same instrument, that is important — say so, and record them as separate series that must never be averaged together.

## Stage 2 — clinical baseline

- What are you dealing with, health-wise? Start wherever makes sense.
- Roughly when did that start?
- Anything you're allergic to, or that you react badly to? What happens?
- Any surgeries or hospital stays worth knowing about?

Family history: ask only if it bears on what they came here to track. Someone tracking sleep does not need a cardiac family history taken.

**Distinguish diagnosed from suspected.** "I have POTS" and "I think I might have POTS" are different records and later analysis depends on which. Ask lightly — "is that a diagnosis, or your read on it?" — and record either without judgment.

## Stage 3 — regimen

- What are you taking? Everything — prescriptions, anything over the counter, supplements, the lot.
- For each: how much, how often?
- Is that on a schedule, or when you need it?

Then:

- Anything you've stopped recently? What happened?
- Anything you take in a bundle — a set of things you always take together?

Bundles are worth capturing as bundles. People take "my bedtime stack," not eleven individual items, and the record should match how they think.

The stopped-recently question is high yield. A drug someone quit because it made them feel terrible is a norm, and often an important one.

## Stage 4 — norms elicitation

Norms cannot be requested. They come out of description. Six moves that work:

### 1. Walk me through a bad day

The single most productive question in the interview. Ask them to narrate one, start to finish. You will get, without asking for any of it: their symptoms, the order those symptoms arrive in, their own words for them, what they do about it, and what they think caused it.

Follow up on sequence specifically — *does that always come before the other thing?* Sequences are the most useful norms and people rarely volunteer them.

### 2. What do you call that?

When they describe something in their own words, stop and pin the word down. People coin private terms for recurring sensations, and those coinages are load-bearing: the word has to be in the norms document, because when they use it later it has to be understood.

Ask directly: *is that your word for it, or is that the medical term?* Both are worth having.

### 3. What already works?

- When that happens, what do you do?
- Does it work? Always, or sometimes?
- Is there a first thing you try and a second thing if that fails?

Escalations matter. "Do X, and if that doesn't work, do Y" is a two-step remedy, and getting it wrong — treating step two as always part of the treatment — produces a wrong reading of every future episode. Capture the order and the trigger for escalating.

### 4. What do you already think causes what?

Ask what they believe, then record it *as a belief*. Do not correct it, do not validate it. "I think the iron supplement sets it off" is a real and useful entry — it tells later analysis what to watch, and it is falsifiable.

Mark these clearly. They are hypotheses under test, not established mechanism.

### 5. What has someone gotten wrong about this?

The best route to the *do-not-infer* norms — rules about what conclusions **not** to draw. People remember being misread, by a doctor, an app, or a family member.

Examples of what this yields: a symptom that looks like anxiety and is not; a number that always reads high on their equipment and does not mean what it appears to; a pattern that looks causal and has been checked and is not.

These are the highest-value norms in the file, and they only come out if you ask for them.

### 6. What's normal for you?

Baselines. A resting heart rate of 85 is unremarkable in someone whose resting heart rate is 85. Ask what their usual is — sleep, energy, how they feel on an ordinary day — so later readings have something to sit against.

## Handling vagueness

Accept it. Record it as given.

- "Some kind of thyroid thing" → record as stated, flag as unconfirmed
- "A while ago, 2019 maybe?" → approximate onset, so noted
- "I don't remember the dose" → record the drug without the dose

Pressing someone for precision they do not have manufactures false precision, and false precision in a health record is worse than a gap. A gap is visible. An invented number is not.

Offer instead: *if you find the bottle later, we can add it.*

## Stopping well

People will stop partway. Handle it so it does not feel like failure:

- Commit what was gathered
- Say plainly what is filled in and what is blank
- Say how to pick it up again
- Do not list everything they did not answer

The most common stopping points are partway through the medication list, which is tedious, and during Stage 4, which is tiring in a different way. Both are fine places to stop. Offer the break before they have to ask for it — *we can do the rest another time, this is already enough to work with.*
